## Inventory what the website depends on

List the domain registrar, DNS, hosting, source repository, CMS, database, email delivery and connected providers. Identify who controls each account and how it can be recovered. A website can remain online while its enquiry destination stops working or its domain renewal fails. Maintenance should cover the business journey, not merely whether the homepage returns a response.

Record the technology, current version and deployment process where known. Separate systems maintained by a provider from configuration your team controls. A hosted service may handle its infrastructure while leaving account permissions and integration settings to the business. Avoid assuming that a platform subscription includes responsibility for everything connected to the site.

## Define updates and change handling

Dependencies, CMS plugins and integrations can require updates. Review compatibility, test important journeys and keep a practical rollback path. Applying every change directly to production without checking its effects is not a maintenance plan. Equally, indefinitely postponing updates creates a different set of operational risks.

Distinguish defect correction from a new feature request. Fixing an agreed form that no longer submits differs from adding a CRM workflow or redesigning a section. State how requests are assessed and authorised. A shared record of changes makes it easier to investigate a later problem and prevents maintenance expectations from expanding without review.

## Treat backups as part of recovery

Determine which files, database records, uploaded assets and configuration are needed to rebuild useful service. Keep backup access separate from the failure it is meant to survive. A copy stored only inside an inaccessible hosting account may not help when that account is the problem. Protect backup data according to its sensitivity.

Test restoration into an appropriate environment and document the steps. Confirm which recent information could be lost and how the business would handle that gap. Recovery targets depend on the system and agreement; do not infer a promise from the presence of a backup button. WordPress's maintenance guidance includes backups, but the actual recovery design must fit your installation.

## Monitor the journeys that matter

A homepage availability check is useful but incomplete. Test important forms, booking flows, logins or checkout paths using a method that does not create unwanted business actions. Confirm the destination receives an enquiry rather than relying only on a success animation. Monitor certificates, domain renewals and provider failures where they affect the agreed scope.

Choose a clear escalation path for alerts. Someone needs to decide whether a signal is actionable and what happens next. Avoid sending every warning to an inbox nobody owns. Record the information needed to investigate without putting passwords or customer messages into general logs. Monitoring should reduce uncertainty, not simply increase the volume of notifications.

## Review content and search foundations

Keep contact details, service information and important policy statements consistent with current business practice. Check links after content changes and preserve useful URLs during restructuring. Metadata, canonical links, sitemap coverage and indexability should be reviewed when new pages are added or old pages are removed.

Measure performance after meaningful changes such as a new analytics script, image-heavy section or integration. A previous Lighthouse result does not guarantee current performance. Review the [new website SEO checklist](/blog/seo-checklist-new-business-website) for launch and maintenance checks. Search work needs useful content and evidence as well as technical correctness, and no maintenance agreement can guarantee rankings.

## Keep access and secrets manageable

Use named accounts and appropriate permissions instead of a single shared administrator login. Remove access when responsibilities change. Store recovery details and credentials securely, with a business owner able to recover critical accounts. A former supplier should not be the only route to renew the domain or deploy a necessary correction.

Review integration permissions and rotate credentials when appropriate. Keep secrets out of source repositories and browser code. If an incident occurs, identify which systems and records are involved before changing access indiscriminately. Specialist security assessments should be scoped explicitly; ordinary maintenance does not imply certification or a guarantee that every vulnerability has been found.

## Compare maintenance proposals by coverage

| Area | Ask what is included | Ask what is excluded |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Updates | Named software and testing process | Redesigns or unsupported extensions |
| Recovery | Backup scope and restoration owner | Unagreed recovery promises |
| Support | Channels, coverage and escalation | Continuous availability unless agreed |

Check who pays hosting, subscriptions and provider charges. Confirm whether the supplier can actually access the systems listed. A proposal can sound comprehensive while omitting a critical third-party form or payment connection. Use the inventory to compare like-for-like coverage rather than choosing solely by a monthly total.

## Plan incidents and handover before you need them

Document how to pause a failing integration, restore a previous release and contact the relevant provider. Keep the steps understandable to the people who will use them. Confirm how the business communicates with customers during an interruption. A technical incident may also need an operational decision about accepting orders or directing enquiries elsewhere.

If the maintenance provider changes, transfer repositories, account roles, configuration notes and known limitations through a controlled handover. Verify access rather than assuming an invitation was accepted. Retain the original acceptance checks for critical journeys. A maintainable site should remain understandable to the next responsible team without depending on a single person's memory.

## Make the agreement practical

Write a scope covering the systems, tasks, reporting and exclusions. Agree support hours and response expectations rather than publishing an unsupported promise. Define how urgent issues differ from planned improvements and who can authorise additional work. Review the agreement when the website gains new integrations or business responsibilities.

Bring the inventory and any current problems to the [website development service](/services/website-development). Explain the consequences of an interruption and the people available to make decisions. That context helps define a proportionate maintenance plan. The aim is dependable operation of an agreed system, with clear evidence and ownership when something changes.


## Retain a useful maintenance record

Keep dates, changes, test results and outstanding limitations in a shared record. The next person responsible for the website should be able to understand what changed and why without reconstructing the history from private messages.
