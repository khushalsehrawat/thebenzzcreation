## Buy when the workflow is already well served

<p>An existing tool can be a good fit for common tasks such as appointment scheduling, standard invoicing or a simple sales pipeline. Trial the actual workflow with representative users and records. Check exports, permissions and integration access on the plan you would purchase, rather than relying on a feature checklist.</p>

## Consider custom work when constraints are structural

<p>A tailored system may make sense when roles, approvals or data relationships repeatedly force workarounds. For example, stock allocation across projects with staged approvals may not fit a basic inventory screen. Document the failed steps and their consequences before deciding that a complete rebuild is necessary.</p>

## Price the whole operating life

<p>Compare subscriptions, per-user fees, integration charges, migration, training and administrative effort with the design, build, hosting and maintenance cost of custom software. Include the cost of changing requirements. A custom product needs someone responsible for updates and incidents after its initial release.</p>

## Test the hardest integration first

<p>Check authentication, API limits, data exports and failure handling. A short technical investigation can reveal whether a planned connection is feasible before a larger build. Avoid assuming that a tool with an API supports every required action or that data can be migrated without reconciliation.</p>

## Use a hybrid approach where it fits

<p>Keep useful systems and add a focused interface or automation around the missing workflow. A dashboard that reads approved records may be enough; replacing the source system adds migration and operational risk. Define which system owns each record and how conflicting updates will be handled.</p>

## Choose a small, testable first step

<p>Write down the task, current bottleneck, users, data sources and a measurable acceptance condition. Review whether configuration, integration or a focused custom release can meet it. Agree security, access, backup and support responsibilities before adopting the result as a business-critical system.</p>

## Use a realistic trial instead of a feature checklist

<p>Choose a representative task with the people who do the work. Include the records, roles, exceptions and outputs they actually need. In a purchasing workflow, that might mean a request, approval, supplier update and reconciliation. A product can list all of those features while handling their relationship differently from your operation.</p><p>During the trial, record where staff leave the system, copy information or work around permissions. Distinguish an unfamiliar interface from a structural gap. Training may solve the first problem; repeatedly changing the meaning of records to fit a tool may indicate the second. Ask the vendor to demonstrate the difficult path using your sample structure, without supplying confidential production data.</p>

## Make a total-cost comparison with explicit assumptions

<p>Include implementation, configuration, subscriptions, integration access, migration, training and ongoing administration. For custom development, include hosting, monitoring, dependency updates and future changes. Neither a low subscription price nor ownership of source code removes operational responsibility. The business needs someone able to maintain access and handle incidents.</p><p>Keep assumptions visible and avoid inventing savings to justify a preferred answer. If staff currently spend time repairing a workflow, measure that effort with a defined method before treating it as a benefit estimate. Compare options over the period the business expects to use them, and identify which costs could change with users, records or provider policies.</p>

## Check exit and migration before committing

<p>Trial a data export and inspect whether it contains the relationships needed to operate elsewhere. A spreadsheet of names may not preserve attachments, permissions, audit history or linked transactions. Ask which formats and API operations are available for the plan being considered. Record any manual reconciliation needed to leave the product.</p><p>For a custom system, confirm repository access, deployment instructions, database documentation and relevant licences. An unfamiliar codebase with no operating notes can create a different form of dependency. Define account ownership and recovery as part of the agreement. Keep a named person responsible for verifying that the business can access and transfer its important records.</p>

## Compare security and operational fit

<p>Identify the data, roles and actions involved before comparing security claims. Check how each option supports access control, account removal, audit records and recovery. Ask for evidence relevant to the actual product and account configuration. A general vendor statement does not establish that your own permissions or data-handling settings are appropriate.</p><p>Determine which responsibilities remain with the business and which belong to the provider or development team. Define support coverage and escalation around the consequences of interruption. Specialist compliance or security assessment should have an explicit scope. Do not infer certification, continuous support or a recovery guarantee from the decision to buy or build.</p>

## Record a decision the next team can understand

<p>Summarise the task, options trialled, evidence and unresolved constraints. Explain why the chosen option supports the essential workflow and what would cause the decision to be reviewed. A hybrid solution may keep a dependable accounting or stock system while adding a focused interface around a missing step.</p><p>Deliver a small complete workflow before committing to a broad replacement. Confirm migration acceptance, operational access and the manual fallback. Use our <a href="/services/custom-software-development">custom software service</a> to investigate a concrete gap rather than to assume a rebuild is necessary. The strongest decision is one that remains understandable when the original project participants are no longer involved.</p>


## A decision workshop for the operating team

<p>Invite the people who enter information, approve changes, handle exceptions and reconcile outputs. Ask each person to describe one task that currently fails and show what a satisfactory result would look like. Group the examples by the record and business rule involved. This avoids building a broad replacement around a complaint that could be solved by a clearer permission or a better export.</p>

<p>Choose one representative example to trial in each credible option. Record the setup required, points of confusion and work remaining outside the product. Include the person responsible for ongoing administration in the review. A tool that only the implementation team understands can create additional support needs after handover, even if its initial demonstration is successful.</p>

<p>Finish with a written decision, unresolved questions and an owner for the next investigation. Do not force a buy-or-build answer when the evidence supports a smaller integration or process correction. Set a review point after actual use so the business can compare expectations with experience. Keep the decision reversible where practical by confirming exports, account access and the ability to stop the pilot without losing important records.</p>
