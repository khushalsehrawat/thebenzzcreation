## Start with a shared business brief

Explain the business task, intended users and outcome the project should support. Include current tools, content, integrations and the reason a change is needed. Separate essential launch requirements from later ideas. A studio can investigate an incomplete brief, but it cannot reliably estimate requirements that are assumed rather than discussed.

Use examples of ordinary work and difficult exceptions. For a booking system, describe cancellations and unavailable slots as well as a successful booking. Identify who approves business rules and who can authorise access to existing systems. Avoid sending passwords or private customer records as part of initial discovery. Documentation and representative sample structures are often enough to start.

## Name decision owners on both sides

Choose a person who can consolidate feedback and make scope decisions for the business. Identify specialists who need to review content, operations or technical constraints. Conflicting feedback from several uncoordinated reviewers can delay a project even when the engineering work is progressing. Record how disagreements will be resolved and which approval is final.

Ask the studio to identify its delivery contact and escalation route. Clarify whether subcontractors or external specialists will be involved where relevant to access and responsibilities. Team size and personal biographies should be supported by actual information, not inferred from polished marketing. A clear working relationship is more useful than an impressive but unverified staffing claim.

## Agree time-zone overlap and asynchronous work

Write down the time zones used for meetings and deadlines. A date without a time zone can mean different things to a team in India and a team in North America. Recheck recurring meetings when participating regions change clocks. Agree an overlap appropriate to the engagement rather than assuming the studio provides continuous availability.

Outside meetings, use a shared place for decisions, questions and review notes. Explain which questions block work and which can wait for the next review. Consolidated written feedback gives the delivery team something stable to implement. An urgent messaging channel should not become the only record of a requirement or approval that affects acceptance later.

## Turn features into acceptance criteria

Describe what a person can do, the data involved and the result that establishes completion. Include validation, access restrictions and recovery from an error. A requirement such as add payments is incomplete without the order state, provider verification, refunds and operational handling. Make assumptions visible before they are embedded in code.

Review criteria with both business and technical owners. Use realistic sample data and identify external dependencies such as provider activation or content approval. Agree how a blocked dependency affects the schedule. A date based on missing access is an assumption, not evidence that the team can deliver the whole workflow by that date.

## Keep accounts and access under business control

Register critical domains, hosting and publishing accounts in a way the business can recover. Invite collaborators through appropriate roles rather than sharing owner credentials. Document billing ownership and the access needed to deploy, monitor and restore the system. Confirm that a change of supplier would not leave the business unable to operate its own product.

Use secure channels for secrets and limit production access to what is needed. Remove access when responsibilities change. Decide which environments and data are suitable for development and testing. Repository access is only one part of control: provider accounts, DNS, databases and release credentials can be equally important during an incident or handover.

## Review complete working journeys

Ask to see progress through the agreed workflow, including backend behaviour and exceptions. A collection of attractive screens does not prove that records are saved correctly or that permissions work. Review on the devices and account roles relevant to actual users. Record the result against the acceptance criteria and identify unresolved issues plainly.

Keep feedback actionable. Explain what happened, what was expected and how to reproduce a problem. Separate defects from a change in business requirements. Approving one part of the system should not silently approve unrelated limitations. A short written review record is easier to use across time zones than a meeting whose decisions remain in participants' memories.

## Handle scope changes explicitly

New information is normal during development. Assess a request for its effects on implementation, testing, dependencies and timing. Decide whether it belongs in the current release, replaces something else or should wait. Avoid treating every request as either automatically included or automatically rejected without reviewing the actual scope.

Keep the current agreement accessible and update the plan after approved changes. Confirm the financial and delivery implications before dependent work starts. If uncertainty is high, a focused investigation may be more appropriate than an immediate fixed commitment. ## Plan data, contracts and invoicing with the right advisers

Agree source-code terms, third-party licences, confidentiality and data-handling responsibilities in the project documents. Identify who approves requirements affecting privacy, billing or regulated operations. A development studio can implement approved controls, but a technical proposal should not be mistaken for legal or tax advice for every market involved.

Confirm invoice currency, accepted payment method, transfer charges and milestone conditions before signing. Provider availability and cross-border arrangements depend on the parties and accounts involved. Do not infer accepted payment methods from a website's location. ## Use a handover checklist before launch

1. Confirm the agreed journeys and unresolved limitations.
2. Verify repository and critical provider account access.
3. Record deployment, recovery and monitoring instructions.
4. Confirm content, licences and source-code terms.
5. Name the operational contact and escalation process.
6. Agree support scope, coverage and future change handling.

Test the handover rather than treating a document as proof of readiness. The responsible team should be able to locate the repository, identify production configuration and follow agreed recovery steps.
