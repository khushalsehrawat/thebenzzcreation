## 1. Decide whether you need a website or an application

<p>A publishing website needs content structure, editing access, enquiries and a maintainable page system. A web application may additionally require accounts, permissions, transactions, dashboards and business integrations. They share a browser but have different delivery and operating responsibilities.</p><p>Describe one complete customer action. For a professional-services website, this might be finding a relevant service and sending an enquiry. For a customer portal, it could involve uploading a document, reviewing status and receiving an approved response. Ask each company to estimate the same journey.</p>

## 2. Verify evidence behind trust claims

<p>Inspect live work on mobile and desktop and ask what the company actually delivered. A screenshot does not establish authorship, backend responsibilities or ongoing reliability. Request permission-based reference calls about projects resembling your own.</p><p>Confirm the people assigned, their availability and how decisions will be recorded across time zones. Country names on a service page do not prove local offices or completed work in those markets. For TheBenzzCreations, the established location is New Delhi, India, with remote enquiries accepted worldwide.</p>

## 3. Require technical SEO and accessibility deliverables

<p>Specify crawlable public pages, descriptive metadata, canonical URLs, sitemap coverage and redirects for moved content. Private account pages need an intentional indexing policy. These are implementation checks; none can guarantee a ranking or AI citation.</p><p>Use <a href="https://www.w3.org/WAI/standards-guidelines/wcag/">W3C WAI: WCAG overview</a> to agree accessibility goals and review representative tasks with keyboard and assistive-technology testing where scoped. Ask for evidence from the delivered build rather than accepting a generic accessibility statement. Performance should also be measured on representative pages and devices.</p>

## 4. Plan editing, integration and regional behavior

<p>Ask who edits pages and how drafts are approved. Test the content workflow before choosing a CMS. Identify contact routing, CRM, payment and booking dependencies and confirm who authorizes each account.</p><p>List language, currency, address and time-zone requirements for launch. Localized content needs its own editorial ownership. Multiple country-targeted pages should offer useful local information rather than repeat the same sales copy with place names changed.</p>

## 5. Compare scope and handover

<p>Request separate costs for design, content, implementation, migration, QA and maintenance. Use the <a href="/blog/website-project-brief">website project brief</a> and <a href="/blog/website-development-cost-2026">website budgeting guide</a> to normalize proposals.</p><p>TheBenzzCreations' <a href="/work">published portfolio</a> offers examples for review. Confirm repository and domain ownership, hosting, backups, support coverage and handover documentation in the proposal before choosing a delivery partner.</p>

## Compare a complete journey, not an agency label

<p>Give each shortlisted team the same scenario and ask how it would move from discovery to a tested release. For example, an enquiry form needs accessible fields, validation, a reliable destination and failure handling. A customer portal adds permissions, account recovery and operational support. A proposal that addresses only the visible screens is not directly comparable with one covering the complete workflow.</p><p>Ask which requirements remain uncertain and how the team would investigate them. A small paid discovery stage may be appropriate where integrations, migration or content are unresolved. Record the output expected from that investigation. Avoid turning an early estimate into a delivery promise before the relevant provider access and business rules have been checked.</p>

## Plan US, UK and UAE reviews concretely

<p>Name the actual time zones for reviewers instead of assuming a single international schedule. US teams may span several regions, UK meeting times can change seasonally relative to India, and UAE projects may need separately scoped Arabic content and right-to-left interfaces. Agree an overlap and a written decision process that fits the people approving work.</p><p>Confirm the business inputs for currency, address formats, invoicing and customer-data handling. Accepted payment methods and transfer charges should be agreed between the parties before signing. Regulatory and tax questions belong with appropriate advisers; a web-development proposal should describe approved technical requirements without presenting itself as legal advice. Our <a href="/global-services">international collaboration guide</a> groups useful planning questions by market.</p>

## Verify the handover before the final approval

<p>Ask the receiving team to identify the repository, production environment, provider accounts and recovery instructions. Check that invitations have been accepted and that the business can recover its own domain and hosting. List any limitations that remain at launch and who is responsible for resolving them.</p><p>Agree how support requests are raised and how a defect differs from a new feature. Keep source-code terms, third-party licences and recurring charges explicit. A clear operational handover is evidence of useful delivery; a vague promise of ongoing help leaves the business dependent on assumptions. Use the <a href="/blog/offshore-development-studio-checklist">offshore studio checklist</a> to review these responsibilities before work begins.</p>


## Ask for evidence you can inspect

<p>Review published work for observable details such as navigation, content hierarchy, accessible controls and usable contact paths. Ask the team to explain its role in the example and distinguish a commissioned project from an internal concept. A screenshot alone does not prove a commercial outcome, a particular backend stack or continuing responsibility for the live system.</p>

<p>Request customer references only through an appropriate permission process. Avoid treating unverified ratings or a claimed client logo as established evidence. Where results are supplied, ask how they were measured and over what period. Keep the decision grounded in information relevant to your scope, while leaving unsupported metrics out of your own business case.</p>
